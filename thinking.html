Thinking Before the Event

Some notes on how we approach event planning and event management — the questions worth asking before the vendors, the venue, and the date lock themselves in.

Before an event becomes an event, someone has to figure out how it's actually going to work. You may already have the date, the venue, a concept, or a brief someone handed you — the hard part is turning that into something that survives contact with the real world.

Maybe you have an idea and need someone to make it real. Maybe you've just been told, at work, that you're now the person responsible for the annual conference or the product launch, and you're not entirely sure where that responsibility starts and ends. Or maybe you started planning something simple — a wedding, a milestone celebration — and watched it quietly become more complicated than you expected, with more vendors, more moving parts, and more people asking you questions you don't have answers to yet.

Wherever you're starting from, the thinking underneath good event planning is mostly the same. It's worth doing before you sign anything.

01

Who Is Actually In Charge?

Most events involve a caterer, a decorator, a sound and lighting vendor, a venue team, sometimes a separate photography and entertainment crew — each competent at their own job, none of them responsible for how the pieces fit together. That gap is where most event-day stress actually comes from.

It shows up in familiar ways: vendors blaming each other when something slips, a timeline that three different people have three different versions of, and — the question that matters most — who do you actually call if something goes wrong at 6pm the day of? If the honest answer is "several different people, depending on what broke," that's not a vendor problem, it's a structure problem. Good event management means one accountable owner holds the whole picture, coordinates every vendor against a single timeline, and is the one number you call, regardless of which piece is misbehaving.

02

The Hidden Cost

The number on the first quote and the number you actually pay are rarely the same number, and that's true across the event industry, not just with any one vendor. It's a well-worn pattern: a headline price that looks reasonable, followed by a string of line items disclosed later — security deposits, corkage or outside-vendor fees, AV markups, service charges and gratuities, taxes, rush charges for last-minute changes.

None of these are necessarily unfair on their own. The problem is timing — finding out about them after you've committed, not before. The fix is asking for an all-in number up front: one document that states what's included, what's excluded, and what triggers an extra charge, before advance money changes hands.

03

Vendor Trust

A polished portfolio tells you what a vendor's best day looked like. It doesn't tell you how they behave on an average day, or a bad one — and that's the day you actually need to plan around.

Before paying an advance to any vendor or agency, it's worth doing a few unglamorous things: asking for references you can actually call, not just quote; getting the scope of work in writing, specific enough that "included" and "not included" aren't left to memory; and asking to see real past work — a full event, ideally one you can verify with the client — rather than a highlight reel. Red flags worth taking seriously: no written contract, reluctance to share references, and pressure to pay the full amount before any of the above is settled.

04

Sequencing & Timeline

A surprising amount of event planning stress comes from booking things in the wrong order. Venues get locked in before the guest count or format is settled. Decor gets briefed before the run-of-show exists. A date gets confirmed before checking whether the vendors you actually want are even free that week.

Each of these causes rework later — a venue that doesn't fit the final numbers, a decor brief that has to be redone once the stage plan changes, a preferred vendor who's already booked. The value of a correct sequence — objective and format first, then date and venue, then the specialist vendors, then the fine detail — isn't just tidiness. It's fewer expensive do-overs in the final weeks, when there's the least room to absorb them.

05

What Can Go Wrong

Rain on an outdoor event. A power or AV failure mid-programme. A vendor who doesn't show. A last-minute change from a client, a couple, or a keynote speaker. None of these are exotic scenarios — they're the ordinary risks of running anything live, and the plan for them is what a contingency plan actually is: not fear-mongering, just the plan for what you hope never happens.

What matters is whether that plan exists before the day, not whether it's ever needed. A backup power source on standby, an indoor fallback for outdoor moments, a vendor bench for no-shows, and someone with the authority to make a call in real time — that's the difference between a hiccup nobody in the audience notices and one that becomes the story of the event.

06

Wedding Thinking, Corporate Thinking

The mechanics of event execution are similar across weddings and corporate events. The pressures underneath them are not.

Weddings

The emotional load is part of the job

A wedding carries family expectations, multi-generational opinions, and a couple who — without help — end up as their own event's unpaid project managers, on top of everything else a wedding already asks of them. Good wedding planning absorbs that load rather than adding to it.

Corporate

Logistics follow the objective, not the other way round

A corporate event needs a clear objective before it needs a venue — what leadership is trying to achieve, who inside the company actually owns delivery across departments, and how visibility and ROI get reported afterward. Corporate events done well start with that question, not with the run sheet.

07

Concept → Reality

Every event starts somewhere as an idea — a dream, a mood board, a one-line brief from a client. The work of event execution is translating that into something a team on the ground can actually build, on a specific date, within a specific budget, with a specific set of vendors.

In practice that translation runs through a fairly consistent sequence: understand what's actually being asked for, challenge the parts of it that won't survive contact with a real venue or a real budget, translate what's left into an execution architecture, build out the specialist ecosystem of vendors that architecture needs, govern the whole thing against one timeline, and arrive at the real event — the version people actually experience.
